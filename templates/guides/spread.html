What Is a Spread?

The great equalizer: a handicap that makes every game a coin flip — almost.

A spread (point spread) is a handicap the sportsbook applies to level the teams. Instead of betting on who wins, you bet on whether a team beats the handicap. The favorite must win by more than the spread; the underdog can lose by less than the spread — or win outright — and still cash.

How it reads

"Chiefs -3.5" means Kansas City must win by 4 or more points. If they win 27-24, they covered — a winning bet. If they win 24-21, they failed to cover, and the bet loses even though they won the game. "Raiders +3.5" is the other side: Las Vegas can lose by 3 and your bet still wins.

That ".5" is called the hook, and it exists to avoid ties. On a whole-number spread like -3, if the favorite wins by exactly 3, the bet is a push — nobody wins, and your stake is returned.

Key numbers

In football, not all points are equal. Games most often end with margins of 3 and 7 (a field goal, a touchdown), so spreads of -3, -7, +3, and +7 are called key numbers. Moving off a key number — say from -3 to -2.5 — is worth real money, which is why books charge extra ("buying points") to move across them. Professionals pay close attention to which side of a key number they're on.

Spreads vs. moneylines

Spreads usually pay close to -110 on both sides, while moneyline prices swing widely. They're two ways of expressing the same opinion about a game. Our model prices the game first and then checks both markets — whichever side of the market offers the real edge is the one we take.

The Sharp Team way: we only play a spread when the edge holds up against the no-vig fair price on that exact line. Research can veto a spread — it can never create one out of thin air.
