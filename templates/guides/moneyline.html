What Is a Moneyline?

The simplest bet in sports: pick the winner. Nothing else matters.

A moneyline bet is a wager on which team wins the game. No point spreads, no margins of victory, no combined scores — just who wins. If your team wins, you win. If they lose, you lose. That's the whole bet.

Reading the price

Moneylines are shown in American odds. The favorite — the team expected to win — carries a negative number, like -150. That means you risk $150 to win $100. The underdog carries a positive number, like +150: risk $100 to win $150.

So when you see "Brewers +125," it means Milwaukee is the underdog: a $100 bet returns $125 in profit if they win. When you see "Dodgers -150," Los Angeles is favored: you'd need to risk $150 to win $100.

Why the number alone means nothing

A +300 underdog isn't a "better bet" than a -110 favorite. The price is just a price tag — the real question is what sits behind it. A +300 needs to win at least 25% of the time to be worth it. A -110 needs to win 52.4% of the time. Without a probability, the odds are decoration.

That's exactly how our system works, in this order: first the model estimates the true chance of winning, then it looks at the price. If the true probability is higher than what the price implies, there's an edge — and only then is it a play.

The Sharp Team way: most of our official plays are moneylines. El Cerebro prices every game before the market is even consulted — the probability comes first, the odds second. Never the other way around.
